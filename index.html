The Practices

Three practices, six ways in.

Each engagement is scoped against a defined outcome and an end date rather than against a title. The same standard runs through all three practices: a single owner per outcome, evidence before sign-off, and a control that sits outside the thing it checks.

i.

Integration & program recovery

Mergers, carve-outs, platform consolidations and programs that are off plan. One integrated plan, one owner per outcome, a dependency register across workstreams, and a governance cadence that surfaces problems while they are still cheap.

ii.

Banking platform modernization

Core banking, commercial lending, mortgage origination. Go-live readiness frameworks where the validation evidence matters as much as the release, and a fixed external date that will not move.

iii.

Transformation office

Standing up a PMO, transformation office or center of excellence: intake, prioritization, governance, service catalog, operating metrics, economics and executive reporting, then handing it to a permanent owner.

iv.

AI adoption operating model

Moving AI from pilot to process. Where the quality gate belongs, how work and roles change, and how to measure productivity end to end rather than on the build phase alone, where the reported number is usually inflated.

v.

AI governance & controls

A fixed-scope AI governance diagnostic built on the cross-framework map: which objectives apply, where controls still depend on a reviewer noticing something looks wrong, and the remediation roadmap.

Findings

Three things worth saying out loud.

Every claim below is tagged with where it comes from. Two decades of enterprise delivery is a different kind of evidence from firsthand operation at n = 1, and conflating them would be the first mistake. Neither proves what happens at ten thousand people.

01.

Built the wrong way round

AI programs are being run as technology programs with a change workstream attached. The evidence I have says they are change programs with a technology workstream. They are built the other way because the people running them came from the technology side.

Grounded in twenty years of enterprise delivery and firsthand operation at n = 1.

02.

The controls quietly weakened

Fluency and correctness have decoupled. Generated work carries none of the tells that uncertain human work carries, so any control that depends on a reviewer noticing something looks wrong is weaker than it was. I have not yet seen this conversation happen between an AI program and a control function.

Grounded in n = 1. Observed directly, not surveyed.

03.

The board number is measured wrong

Productivity is typically measured on the build phase, which compresses dramatically. The last mile does not compress at all. Measured end to end, the effect is real and materially smaller than the build figure suggests, which is why year-two disappointment is so predictable.

Grounded in n = 1, observed across the full build, ship and operate cycle.

Operating Notes

What held across every release.

The findings above came out of running a complete AI adoption arc firsthand rather than reading about one. These four held from the first release to the sixth, and none of them are what the tooling vendors say. The full write-up, including what broke and what I would do differently, is in Six Products, One Operator.

Where it holds

Scaffolding, boilerplate, refactors, test generation, and first drafts of anything with a known structure. The compression is not incremental. AI dramatically compressed production.

Where it breaks

Judgment about what to build rather than how. Architecture, data model tradeoffs, pricing, and whether a feature should exist at all. The failure mode is confident and wrong, and the artifact gives you no signal either way.

Where the gate belongs

Before generation, at the point of intent, and again at the boundary where work touches something a user depends on. Reviewing generated work at the end of the pipeline can cost more than writing it by hand.

Why adoption stalls

At the last mile. Everything between a working capability and a changed way of operating: existing process, accountability, and the people who own the outcome. Capability arrives long before behavior does.
